Packing snacks, spices, or a jar of your favorite hot sauce for your trip to Korea?
Some foods sail through Korean customs without a second look — others get confiscated on the spot.
Here’s exactly what you can bring, what’s restricted, and how to declare it properly.

Can You Bring Food Into Korea? The Short Answer

Yes, you can bring food into Korea, but with real limits: most commercially packaged, non-meat snacks and processed foods are fine in reasonable personal-use amounts, while fresh fruit, vegetables, raw meat, dairy, and anything containing soil or live organisms are restricted or banned outright. Whatever falls into a restricted category must be declared on arrival — even if it’s well within the allowed weight and value limits.

Korea’s customs and quarantine rules exist to keep invasive pests and animal diseases out of the country, not simply to inconvenience travelers. If you’re not sure whether something you’re carrying counts, the safest move is to declare it and let a customs or quarantine officer make the call — not to guess and risk confiscation or a fine. For how this fits into the rest of the arrival process, see our guide on how immigration and customs work in Korea.

  • Generally fine in reasonable amounts: commercially sealed snacks, coffee, tea, chocolate, and other non-meat processed foods
  • Restricted or subject to inspection: fresh fruit and vegetables, raw or processed meat, dairy, seeds, soil, live plants, and live seafood
  • Always required: declaring any item in a restricted category on your arrival card, regardless of quantity

📌 Quick gut check
If it’s fresh, raw, alive, or made from meat or dairy, assume it needs to be declared — and possibly inspected — before you find out the hard way.

What Foods and Agricultural Products Are Restricted When Entering Korea?

Korea restricts a specific set of categories to protect its agriculture, livestock, and food supply from pests and animal diseases. According to the Korea Customs Service, the following are restricted from entry and require quarantine clearance:

  • Plants, fruits, and vegetables
  • Agricultural, poultry, and forestry products
  • Animal products, including meat, skin, and fur
  • Livestock
  • Live sea creatures, such as live fish and shellfish

Korea Customs Service’s own declaration guidance goes a step further than just raw ingredients: it groups meat, skin, and hair together with plants, fruit, vegetables, and live fish, and adds “agricultural, forest, livestock, and fishery products (including processed goods)” and “other foods” to the list of items travelers must declare. In plain terms, dried, cured, canned, or otherwise processed versions of these products — think beef jerky, sausages, or packaged seafood — fall under the same declaration requirement as fresh ones, even though they’re not raw.

Korea’s Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA) breaks the declarable list down further by product type. On the animal side, that includes beef, pork, lamb, and chicken; processed meats like ham, sausage, jerky, and canned meat; bone-based products such as horns and animal bones; egg products (whole eggs, egg white, powdered egg); and dairy like milk, cheese, and butter. On the plant side, nearly all fresh fruits and vegetables are restricted, along with rice, rice bran, and straw-based products, unpeeled walnuts, unripe soybeans, unpeeled potatoes and sweet potatoes, wild ginseng, cultivated mushrooms, and saplings or cuttings from apple, pear, grape, or pine trees.

⚠️ Double-check before you pack
Fresh fruit, home-dried or cured meat, plants or hiking gear with soil still attached, and unshelled nuts or seeds all fall under the restricted categories above — pack them expecting inspection, or leave them out.

Food isn’t the only thing with its own packing rules — spare batteries and power banks are capped separately under Korea’s 100Wh/160Wh carry-on rule. See our spare batteries and power banks guide if you’re flying with electronics.

How Much Food Can You Bring Into Korea Duty-Free?

Korea’s general personal duty-free allowance for travelers is US$600, but agricultural, livestock, and fishery products are governed by a separate, more specific allowance, according to the Korea Customs Service.

To qualify for duty-free treatment, agricultural and food items must meet three conditions at the same time: the total weight must stay under 40kg, the total overseas purchase price must stay under 100,000 KRW (roughly USD 75), and the items must pass quarantine inspection. Within that overall cap, specific items also have their own individual weight limits:

ItemDuty-Free Limit
Sesame oil5kg
Honey5kg
Pine nuts1kg
Beef10kg
Pork10kg
Bracken (fern) and deodeok5kg
Other agricultural/food items5kg each
Ginseng (fresh, white, or red)300g
Deer antlers150g
Other oriental medicine materials3kg each

⚠️ Exceeding a limit can cost you more than just the excess.
Per the Korea Customs Service, going over the weight or value threshold for an item can make the entire quantity — not just the amount above the limit — subject to duty. When in doubt, bring less, or declare it and ask.

Do You Need to Declare Food at Korean Customs?

Yes — if you’re carrying any food or agricultural item from the restricted categories above, you must declare it, regardless of whether it falls within the duty-free limits. Declaration and duty-free treatment are two separate questions: being under the weight and value caps means you likely won’t pay duty, but it doesn’t excuse you from declaring the item in the first place.

In practice, this happens through the same e-Arrival Card and Q-code process used for the rest of your customs declaration. If you haven’t seen how that process works end-to-end, our guide on how immigration and customs work in Korea covers the full arrival-card and Q-code flow; the food and agricultural section is simply one part of that same declaration.

How Food Declaration Fits Into Arrival

  1. Fill in the food/agricultural item section of your e-Arrival Card (or paper declaration form) before or during your flight — see our visa and K-ETA guide for how the e-Arrival Card itself works
  2. Follow signage for the declaration (red) channel if you’re carrying any item that needs to be declared
  3. Present the item(s) to a customs or quarantine officer for inspection when asked
  4. Pay any applicable duty, or have the item confiscated on the spot if it’s prohibited or fails inspection

What Happens If You Don’t Declare Food or Bring Prohibited Items?

You’ll face a financial penalty at minimum, and confiscation or legal trouble at worst. The Korea Customs Service applies an additional duty surcharge to any passenger who fails to voluntarily declare goods that should have been declared — 40% on a first offense, rising to 60% for repeated failures to declare. This general declaration-surcharge rule covers the food and agricultural categories above, since Customs Service guidance already lists them among items travelers must declare.

Separately, items that are outright prohibited or restricted from entry — including undeclared plants, fruit, meat, and other agricultural or animal products — are confiscated when discovered. Korean customs law can also impose punishment on travelers found carrying a prohibited or restricted item during a customs investigation, on top of losing the item itself.

🚫 Undeclared isn’t a shortcut.
Skipping the declaration doesn’t just risk losing the item — it adds a 40–60% duty surcharge on top, and repeated or serious cases can lead to further penalties under customs law. Declaring costs you a few extra minutes; not declaring can cost a lot more.

Are There Extra Restrictions on Meat During Animal Disease Outbreaks?

Yes, and these restrictions can change with little notice. Korea’s Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA) and Customs Service periodically tighten or fully ban imports of meat, dairy, and other livestock products from specific countries or regions during active outbreaks of animal diseases such as African Swine Fever or avian influenza — on top of the general restrictions already covered above.

Because which countries are affected changes as outbreaks are reported and resolved, this article can’t list a fixed set of banned countries or products. If you’re traveling from a region with a recent or ongoing animal disease outbreak, call APQA’s animal quarantine import line directly at +82-32-740-2671 before you pack any meat, dairy, or livestock-derived product — the safest assumption is that country-specific bans can apply on top of the standard rules.

📌 Rules can change by country and by outbreak.
Always check the current status for your departure country on the official APQA or Korea Customs Service site rather than relying on what was true for a previous trip.

Practical Tips: What to Pack and What to Leave Behind

The lowest-risk approach is to stick to commercially packaged, shelf-stable food and leave fresh or homemade items at home. Sealed, factory-packaged snacks, coffee, tea, and confectionery generally move through customs smoothly in personal-use quantities, especially when they don’t contain meat or dairy as a primary ingredient.

  • Pack it with confidence: commercially sealed, shelf-stable snacks, instant coffee, tea, and packaged confectionery
  • Declare it and expect inspection: dried or cured meat, packaged seafood, honey, sesame oil, and other items on the duty-free limit table above
  • Leave it behind if you can: fresh fruit and vegetables, raw or vacuum-packed meat, unpasteurized dairy, uncooked seafood, and any plant with soil still attached

✅ When in doubt, declare it.
A declared item that turns out to be fine costs you a short conversation with a customs officer. An undeclared item that turns out to be restricted costs you the item, plus a duty surcharge.

Korea Customs Service Call Center

  • Phone Number: 125 (toll-free, nationwide)
  • Use this line for questions about declaration requirements, duty-free allowances, and prohibited/restricted items

Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA)

  • Animal quarantine (meat, dairy, egg, and other animal products): +82-32-740-2671 (bringing items into Korea) / +82-32-740-2660~1 (taking items out of Korea)
  • Plant quarantine (fruit, vegetables, seeds, saplings, and other plant products): +82-32-740-2079 (bringing items into Korea) / +82-32-740-2077 (taking items out of Korea)

FAQ: Bringing Food Into Korea Questions Answered

Can you bring food into Korea?

Yes, in general. Commercially packaged, non-meat snacks and processed foods are usually fine in personal-use amounts, while fresh fruit, vegetables, raw meat, dairy, and anything with soil or live organisms are restricted and must be declared.

Can I bring fresh fruit into Korea?

Fresh fruit falls under Korea’s restricted agricultural categories and requires quarantine inspection on arrival. It must be declared, and it may be confiscated depending on the fruit and its origin.

Can I bring meat, like beef or pork, into Korea?

In limited amounts, yes — beef and pork each have a 10kg duty-free allowance under Korea Customs Service rules, as long as the total agricultural weight stays under 40kg, the value stays under 100,000 KRW, and the meat passes quarantine inspection. It must still be declared regardless of quantity, and additional country-specific bans can apply during animal disease outbreaks.

Do I have to declare food even if it’s within the duty-free limit?

Yes. Staying within the weight and value limits generally means you won’t owe duty, but it doesn’t remove the requirement to declare the item — declaration and duty-free treatment are separate questions.

What happens if I don’t declare food at Korean customs?

You’ll face an additional duty surcharge — 40% for a first-time failure to declare, 60% for repeated cases — and any prohibited or restricted item can be confiscated, with possible further penalties under Korean customs law.

Are processed or packaged foods treated differently from fresh food?

Not entirely. Korea Customs Service’s declaration guidance explicitly includes “agricultural, forest, livestock, and fishery products (including processed goods),” so items like jerky, sausages, or packaged seafood still need to be declared, even though they’re less likely to be outright prohibited than fresh or raw versions.

Can I bring ginseng or other traditional Korean ingredients back with me?

Yes, within specific limits — for example, fresh, white, or red ginseng has a 300g duty-free allowance, and deer antlers are capped at 150g, with other oriental medicine materials generally allowed up to 3kg each.

Where can I check the latest country-specific restrictions on meat and animal products?

Check the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA) official site, or call their animal quarantine import line at +82-32-740-2671, before you travel — restrictions tied to animal disease outbreaks like African Swine Fever or avian influenza can change by country with little notice.

What specific animal and plant products do I need to declare?

On the animal side: beef, pork, lamb, and chicken; processed meats like ham, sausage, jerky, and canned meat; bone-based products such as horns and animal bones; egg products; and dairy like milk, cheese, and butter. On the plant side: nearly all fresh fruits and vegetables, rice and rice-based products, unpeeled walnuts, unripe soybeans, unpeeled potatoes and sweet potatoes, wild ginseng, cultivated mushrooms, and saplings or cuttings from fruit or pine trees.

Still not sure whether something in your bag needs to be declared? Call the Korea Customs Service at 125 (toll-free, nationwide) before you fly, or declare it on arrival and let an officer make the call.
