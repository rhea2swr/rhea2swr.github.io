How Many Spare Batteries or Power Banks Can You Bring on a Flight to Korea? (100Wh vs 160Wh Rule)

Packing a power bank for your Korea trip, or bringing a couple of spare camera batteries for a long day of sightseeing?
Korea’s airport security enforces a strict, watt-hour-based limit on spare lithium batteries — and getting it wrong can mean losing the item at the gate.
Here’s exactly how many spare batteries or power banks you can bring on a flight to or from Korea, and how to pack them correctly.

Can You Bring Spare Batteries or Power Banks on a Flight to Korea?

Yes — spare lithium-ion batteries and power banks are allowed on flights to and from Korea, but only within specific watt-hour (Wh) and quantity limits set under Korea’s Aviation Security Act. This rule has applied to all international flights departing from or arriving in Korea (including transfer passengers) since March 1, 2007, and it’s enforced the same way whether you’re flying out of Incheon, Gimpo, Busan, or any other Korean airport.

In short: you can carry up to 5 spare batteries or power banks rated 100Wh or less, plus up to 2 more rated between 100Wh and 160Wh — but nothing above 160Wh is allowed at all, in either carry-on or checked baggage.

📌 The quick answer
• Up to 5 spare batteries ≤100Wh — carry-on only
• Up to 2 spare batteries >100Wh–160Wh — carry-on only
• Anything over 160Wh is prohibited entirely — not allowed on the plane at all

What Is the 100Wh vs 160Wh Lithium Battery Rule?

The 100Wh vs 160Wh rule is a tiered limit on how many “spare” (uninstalled) lithium-ion batteries — including portable chargers, power banks, and loose camera or drone batteries — a single passenger can bring onto a flight to or from Korea, based on each battery’s watt-hour (Wh) rating.

Renting a WiFi egg or pocket WiFi router for your trip? Those run on the same kind of lithium-ion battery, so the same spare-battery carry-on rules apply to them too — see our SIM card, eSIM, and WiFi egg guide for how to pick one.

Watt-hour (Wh) ratingMax quantity per passengerWhere it’s allowed
100Wh or lessUp to 5 spare batteriesCarry-on only
Over 100Wh, up to 160WhUp to 2 spare batteriesCarry-on only
Over 160WhNot allowedProhibited — carry-on and checked baggage

Most standard power banks sold for travel (roughly 10,000–20,000mAh) fall well under the 100Wh threshold, so the 5-battery limit covers the vast majority of travelers. The 100–160Wh tier is mainly relevant to larger camera, drone, or professional equipment batteries. Batteries above 160Wh — such as certain large drone or e-bike batteries — aren’t permitted on passenger flights to or from Korea under any circumstances, so those need to be shipped separately if you need to bring them at all.

How Do You Check Your Battery’s Watt-Hour (Wh) Rating?

You check your battery’s Wh rating by looking for it printed directly on the battery or power bank casing — most manufacturers print Wh right on the label, often next to the mAh and voltage figures. If only milliamp-hours (mAh) and voltage (V) are listed, you can estimate the watt-hours yourself.

Wh Formula (If Only mAh and V Are Listed)

Watt-hours = (mAh ÷ 1,000) × Voltage. For reference, most portable chargers use a nominal voltage around 3.6–3.7V, so a 10,000mAh power bank works out to roughly 37Wh, and a 20,000mAh power bank to roughly 74Wh — both comfortably under the 100Wh limit. Once a power bank’s rated capacity climbs toward 25,000–27,000mAh, it can start approaching the 100Wh line, so it’s worth checking the exact label rather than estimating.

⚠️ No Wh rating printed anywhere?
If you can’t find or calculate the watt-hour rating, airline staff or security screeners may deny the battery at the gate rather than guess. When in doubt, check the manufacturer’s product page or manual before you travel — don’t rely on packaging that only shows mAh.

How Should You Pack Spare Batteries and Power Banks?

You should pack all spare batteries and power banks in your carry-on bag, never in checked luggage — this applies regardless of Wh rating, since spare lithium batteries in checked baggage pose a fire risk in the cargo hold where it can’t be quickly detected. Korean aviation security rules also require that spare batteries stay accessible to you throughout the flight, not stored away in the overhead bin.

  1. Confirm each spare battery’s Wh rating before you pack, and count how many fall in the ≤100Wh and 100–160Wh tiers
  2. Pack all spare batteries and power banks in your carry-on bag only — never in checked luggage
  3. Protect each battery’s terminals from short-circuiting: use an individual plastic bag, a dedicated battery case or pouch, tape over the terminals, or a terminal safety cap
  4. Keep spare batteries easily accessible for the security screening tray, separate from the rest of your carry-on if asked
  5. During the flight, keep spare batteries on your person or in the seat-back pocket — not in the overhead bin

⚠️ Loose batteries with exposed terminals can be confiscated.
An unprotected battery bouncing around in your bag risks short-circuiting against keys, coins, or other metal objects. Security staff can refuse to let it through if the terminals aren’t protected — even if the Wh rating and quantity are within the allowed limits.

What About Batteries Built Into Your Phone, Laptop, or Camera?

Batteries that are installed inside a device you’re actively carrying — like your phone, laptop, tablet, or camera — are treated differently from loose “spare” batteries, since they’re not counted against the 5-battery or 2-battery spare limits. As a general aviation safety practice, it’s still best to keep devices with built-in lithium batteries in your carry-on rather than checked luggage, since airlines and security authorities generally prefer battery-powered electronics to stay in the cabin where any issue can be spotted immediately.

Rules can vary by airline and route, so if you’re traveling with unusually large equipment — professional camera batteries, drone batteries, or medical devices with high-capacity batteries — it’s worth confirming directly with your airline before departure, in addition to checking the official guidance below.

Pre-Flight Battery & Carry-On Checklist

Spare batteries are just one item on Korea’s restricted carry-on list — run through this checklist before you head to the airport to avoid any last-minute surprises at security.

  • All spare batteries and power banks are in your carry-on bag, not checked luggage
  • You’ve counted no more than 5 spare batteries ≤100Wh and 2 spare batteries between 100–160Wh
  • None of your spare batteries exceed 160Wh
  • Battery terminals are protected with a bag, case, tape, or terminal cap
  • Liquids, gels, and aerosols are each ≤100ml and packed in one clear zip-top bag
  • Any items exceeding Korea’s duty-free allowance are ready to declare on arrival

The battery rule is just one part of a longer list of items that are prohibited or restricted from entering or leaving Korea — everything from weapons and protected wildlife products to cash over $10,000 falls under separate customs rules. FAQ: Batteries and Power Banks on Flights to Korea

How many spare batteries or power banks can I bring on a flight to Korea?

Up to 5 spare batteries or power banks rated 100Wh or less, plus up to 2 more rated between 100Wh and 160Wh — for a possible maximum of 7 spare batteries in total, all carried in your cabin baggage.

What happens if my battery is over 160Wh?

It’s prohibited entirely — you cannot bring a spare battery over 160Wh in either carry-on or checked baggage on a flight to or from Korea. If you need to transport equipment with a battery that large, you’ll need to look into a separate cargo or shipping option.

Can I pack spare batteries in my checked luggage?

No. Spare lithium-ion batteries and power banks must go in your carry-on bag, and must stay on your person or in the seat-back pocket during the flight — never in the overhead bin and never in checked luggage.

How do I find my battery’s watt-hour (Wh) rating if it’s not printed?

Check the manufacturer’s product page or manual, or calculate it yourself: Wh = (mAh ÷ 1,000) × Voltage. If you genuinely can’t determine the rating, airline staff may refuse the item rather than guess, so it’s worth confirming before you travel.

Do laptop, phone, or camera batteries count under this rule?

Batteries installed inside a device you’re carrying (not loose spares) aren’t counted against the 5- or 2-battery spare limits. It’s still good practice to keep any battery-powered electronics in your carry-on rather than checked luggage.

Does this rule apply only to flights leaving Korea, or also flights arriving?

It applies both ways — to all international flights departing from and arriving in Korea, including passengers transferring through a Korean airport, under Korea’s Aviation Security Act.

Can airport security take away my power bank if it doesn’t meet the rule?

Yes. A power bank that exceeds the Wh or quantity limits, or that’s packed in checked luggage, or has unprotected terminals, can be confiscated at security or refused by the airline at the gate.

Are there other carry-on restrictions I should know before flying to Korea?

Yes — liquids, gels, and aerosols are capped at 100ml per container and must fit in one clear zip-top bag, and a longer list of prohibited/restricted items (weapons, certain plants and animal products, cash over $10,000, and more) applies at Korean customs.
